# BASIC

- Year: 1964
- Designer(s): John Kemeny, Thomas Kurtz
- Paradigm(s): procedural
- Family: origin

## Problem It Aimed to Solve

John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz of Dartmouth College were troubled by the fact that programming at the time was a skill limited to specialists in science and engineering. They wanted to create an environment where students from any major, including the humanities, could learn programming interactively and immediately from a terminal. Existing languages such as Fortran and ALGOL 60 assumed batch processing, and the time required for compiling and submitting jobs made them intimidating for beginners.

They developed BASIC, which had a simple grammar and offered an interactive environment providing immediate execution results on the Dartmouth Time-Sharing System, and made it available free of charge to all students at the university. This philosophy of "anyone can start writing code right away" was directly inherited by the personal computer culture that followed.

## Features

- A simple program structure based on line numbers (PRINT, GOTO, INPUT, etc.)
- Designed on the assumption of interactive execution on a time-sharing system
- Plain, easy-to-learn grammar accessible even to beginners without specialized training
- Became a standard feature of personal computers from the late 1970s through the 1980s, spreading explosively
- Started out as an educational tool but built a culture of hobbyist programming
- Heavy reliance on the GOTO statement later became a target of criticism as "unstructured"

## Current Status

BASIC is now positioned as a legacy language (status: legacy). It was a key driver of the 1980s personal computer boom, and its design philosophy has been carried forward into many derivative languages and environments through Visual Basic and others.

Few of the original BASIC dialects themselves are still used in active practice today, but its role as an introductory language for programming education has been inherited by later educational languages.
