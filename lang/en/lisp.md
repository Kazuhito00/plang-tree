# Lisp

- Year: 1958
- Designer(s): John McCarthy
- Paradigm(s): functional, symbolic processing
- Family: Lisp/Scheme family

## Problem It Aimed to Solve

In the late 1950s, artificial intelligence research needed not just numerical computation but also the ability to manipulate lists of symbols while performing theorem proving and natural language processing. Fortran, the dominant language of the time, was strong at numerical computation but poorly suited to expressing variable-length list structures or recursive symbolic manipulation. John McCarthy sought to implement, as an actual working system, the theory of recursive functions based on the mathematical lambda calculus, creating a language that could treat symbolic processing and programs themselves as data. The Lisp that resulted became the second-oldest high-level language after Fortran.

## Features

- Programs and data are represented in the same form (code is data) through list structures made of parentheses, called S-expressions
- One of the very first languages to place recursive function calls and lambda expressions at its core
- One of the earliest languages to implement garbage collection
- Powerful metaprogramming through macros that can generate and transform programs themselves
- Became the common foundation that gave rise to numerous later dialects (Scheme, Common Lisp, and others)

## Current Status

Lisp itself is now a "historical" language rarely used directly in practice, but ideas such as S-expressions, recursion, garbage collection, and metaprogramming have been passed down to countless later languages, and it is considered one of the most influential designs in the history of programming languages.
