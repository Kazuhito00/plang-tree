# Logo

- Year: 1967
- Designer(s): Seymour Papert and others
- Paradigm(s): educational, functional
- Family: educational-visual

## Problem It Aimed to Solve

The psychologist and mathematician Seymour Papert wondered whether children could intuitively grasp abstract mathematical concepts through a "bodily sense of movement." Programming education at the time was aimed mainly at university students and professionals, and there was almost no language accessible to children.

Building on Lisp's approach to symbolic processing, Papert introduced "turtle graphics," in which a character called a "turtle" on screen is moved by commands to draw shapes, and designed Logo as a language for learning procedural thinking, recursion, and geometric concepts while playing. This idea grew out of educational experiments at MIT's Artificial Intelligence Laboratory.

## Features

- Its core feature is "turtle graphics," in which a turtle on screen moves forward and turns to draw shapes
- Has a functional side, supporting list processing and recursive calls inherited from Lisp
- Designed to be forgiving of mistakes, with an educational design that allows learning through trial and error
- Users can define their own procedures, name them, and reuse them
- Based on the educational theory of "microworlds," in which concepts are learned by manipulation within a limited world
- Despite being aimed at children's education, it also has the features of a full-fledged programming language

## Current Status

Logo, as the originator of turtle graphics, left a large mark on the history of educational languages, and it continues to be used in some educational settings today.

It is positioned as a language that laid the foundation for the field of programming education itself, with its ideas carried forward into more intuitive visual programming environments such as Scratch that followed it.
