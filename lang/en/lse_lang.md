# LSE

- Year: 1971
- Designer(s): Yves Noyelle, Stéphane Berche, Jacques Hebenstreit
- Paradigm(s): procedural
- Family: origin

## Problem It Aimed to Solve

In early-1970s France, there was a need for an interactive language with French-language keywords for teaching programming in schools. Engineers at Supélec (the École Supérieure d'Électricité) developed the predecessor language LSD further, designing it as an educational language that combined BASIC-like ease of use with procedures and functions built in from the start. In 1972 the French Ministry of Education decided to adopt it, and it was widely deployed in French secondary schools via minicomputers such as the CII Mitra 15 and the Télémécanique T1600.

## Features

- Adopts French-based keywords such as IMPRIME (print) and SI...ALORS (if...then), letting procedures be written in the learner's native language
- An interactive (conversational) processing system that executes entered commands immediately and gives feedback on the results
- Retained BASIC-like simplicity while building procedures (subroutines) and functions into the language specification from the outset
- Adopted a line-numbering scheme, giving it an educational design whose structure is easy for beginners to grasp
- Ran on minicomputers of the era such as the CII Mitra 15 and the Télémécanique T1600
- Adopted into the official curriculum of the French Ministry of Education and widely used in secondary education

## Current Status

LSE is a historical language that has finished its role, and it is no longer used in practice today. It is recorded in the context of educational and computing history as a language that supported the early days of computer science education in France.
