# NetLogo

- Year: 1999
- Designer(s): Uri Wilensky
- Paradigm(s): procedural, concurrent, visual
- Family: educational-visual

## Problem It Aimed to Solve

NetLogo was developed by Uri Wilensky at Northwestern University so that scientists and students without programming experience could build multi-agent simulations of complex systems. Existing languages such as Logo and StarLogo made it difficult to intuitively describe large-scale, concurrent agent-based modeling, and there was demand for a language with a "low floor and high ceiling" that could serve both educational and research settings. NetLogo introduced the agent concepts of turtles, patches, links, and observers, making it possible to visualize and analyze emergent phenomena across a wide range of fields including economics, biology, and physics. It continues to be used in both education and simulation research today, appearing in thousands of academic papers.

## Features

- Centers on four kinds of agent concepts: turtles (mobile agents), patches (the grid cells that make up space), links (connections between agents), and the observer (the overarching viewpoint)
- Built on procedural syntax derived from Logo, yet capable of describing simulations that run thousands to millions of agents concurrently
- Supports automated experiments that vary parameters through tools such as BehaviorSpace, with statistical analysis of the results
- Ships with a "models library" spanning many fields—economics, ecology, social science, physics—that learners can study and modify
- Supports browser-based execution via NetLogo Web and 3D rendering, reflecting a design aimed at classroom use

## Current Status

NetLogo remains an actively used language today, continuing to see vigorous use in both academic research and education. It is used in the multi-agent simulation of complex systems in thousands of peer-reviewed papers, and it has established itself as a standard educational simulation tool. It is likely to remain one of the standard tools in the field of complex systems science going forward.
