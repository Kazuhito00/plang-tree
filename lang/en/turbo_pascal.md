# Turbo Pascal

- Year: 1983
- Designer(s): Anders Hejlsberg
- Paradigm(s): procedural, object-oriented
- Family: algol-pascal

## Problem It Aimed to Solve

In the early 1980s, programming required the cumbersome use of separate tools—an editor, a compiler, a linker—and integrated development environments were extremely expensive. Philippe Kahn of Borland conceived of bundling these functions into a single, inexpensive integrated development environment (IDE), and Anders Hejlsberg implemented a system built around an extremely fast one-pass compiler. A remarkably low price of $49.95, combined with an edit-compile cycle that felt comfortable even on the slow machines of the day, spread serious Pascal development to individuals and educational institutions alike. As a result, it became the standard development environment for individuals and education during the MS-DOS era.

## Features

- Provided an integrated development environment (IDE) bundling an editor, compiler, and linker as a low-priced package
- Its extremely fast one-pass compiler delivered a comfortable edit-compile-run cycle even on the slow machines of the day
- Added practical extensions to standard Pascal such as units (modules) and an inline assembler
- Supported object-oriented programming (classes, inheritance) from version 5.5 onward
- Its affordable price and ease of use spread it widely, from MS-DOS-era hobbyists to commercial developers

## Current Status

Turbo Pascal is a legacy presence and is rarely adopted for new work in current development. However, part of its design lives on today through its successor, Object Pascal (Delphi), and its historical significance in popularizing the very concept of the integrated development environment is considerable.

## Hello World

Following standard Pascal syntax, the code is written as a `begin`–`end.` block after a `program` declaration.

```
program HelloWorld;
begin
  writeln('Hello, world.')
end.
```
